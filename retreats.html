Healing retreats & programs

Where ayahuasca — the DMT brew — is used as a healing process, what the evidence says, and how to tell a careful center from a dangerous one.

The vetting checklist Retreat directory

Read this first

Nothing on this page is a recommendation to drink ayahuasca or an endorsement of any center. Retreats are largely unregulated: Peru's government does not license them, and in January 2025 both the U.S. Embassy in Peru and Costa Rica's Ministry of Health issued public warnings after participants died, became seriously ill, or were sexually assaulted or robbed at “healing” centers.2,3 No retreat can guarantee your safety. If you are on any medication, or have a heart condition or a personal or family history of psychosis or bipolar disorder, talk to your own doctor before going any further.

Researched and last reviewed: November 2026. Centers change owners, staff and practices — always verify for yourself.

Orientation

What a “healing retreat” actually is

Almost every DMT-based healing setting uses ayahuasca — the slow, hours-long brew — not smoked DMT. But the containers around it are very different, and the differences matter for safety and for the law.

Traditional ceremonial retreats

Usually led by Indigenous healers — most often Shipibo onanyabo from the Peruvian Amazon — who sing healing songs (icaros) through night-time ceremonies. Many include a dieta: restricted food, isolation and other “teacher plants.” Typically 1–3 weeks.

Therapeutic communities

A small number of centers fold ayahuasca into structured, months-long treatment — most notably addiction rehabilitation — with psychologists working alongside traditional healers.

Ayahuasca churches

Religions such as Santo Daime and the União do Vegetal (UDV) use ayahuasca as a sacrament. They are legal in Brazil and hold church-specific exemptions in the U.S. and Canada — but they are faith communities with commitments, not retreats you book.

Clinical trials

Hospital and university studies of ayahuasca and pure DMT for depression and anxiety. The only setting with full medical oversight and formal ethics review — and the source of the strongest evidence. See legal routes.

The evidence

Does retreat-based healing work?

The honest answer: there are real signals of benefit, the research is thin, and harms are under-counted.

01

Encouraging signals

Observational studies of retreat participants report improvements in depression, PTSD and substance use. The Ayahuasca Treatment Outcome Project (ATOP), for example, follows addiction patients for up to two years after leaving treatment.1

02

But no control groups

Retreat studies follow self-selected people who chose and paid to go, usually without a comparison group, and are sometimes funded by the centers or programs being studied. They show what's possible, not what's proven.

03

The strongest evidence is clinical

The placebo-controlled results — a 2019 ayahuasca trial and a 2026 trial of intravenous DMT for depression5 — came from screened patients in medical settings. Those results don't automatically transfer to a jungle lodge. See the science.

04

Harms go unreported

There is no central system for recording deaths, psychiatric crises or abuse at retreats. What we know comes from news reports, embassy warnings and survivor testimony, so the true rate is unknown.

Due diligence

The vetting checklist: 12 questions to ask

Ask every one of these in writing before you pay a deposit. A good center answers them easily. Vague, defensive or pressuring replies are your answer.

01

How will you screen me?

Expect a detailed medical and psychiatric questionnaire, a medication review, and a conversation with someone medically trained. A center that turns nobody away is not screening.

02

What's the plan for my medications?

Ayahuasca contains MAO inhibitors that can react dangerously with antidepressants and other drugs. The center should flag this — but any change to your medication must be planned with your own prescriber, never on a retreat's say-so.

03

Who is medically qualified on site — at night?

Ceremonies happen after dark. Ask whether a nurse or doctor is present during ceremonies, and whether there is a defibrillator and oxygen.

04

What's the emergency plan?

How far is the nearest hospital, how long does it take to get there at 3 a.m., and is transport available 24 hours? Get it in writing, and check your travel insurance covers you.

05

Who leads the ceremonies?

Ask for names, lineage and years of training, and how long they have worked with this center. Be wary of facilitators with a few months of experience.

06

How many staff per participant?

Ask about group size and how many sober helpers are in the room. One healer for thirty people cannot watch everyone.

07

What is your touch and consent policy?

Sexual abuse by healers and facilitators is a documented problem.4 Good centers have a written no-sexual-contact rule, keep healers within view of other staff, and tell you how to report a violation.

08

What exactly is in the cup — and what else is on the schedule?

Traditional ayahuasca is the vine (Banisteriopsis caapi) plus a DMT-containing leaf. Ask about admixtures such as toé (brugmansia), which is dangerous. Stacking other substances — kambo, 5-MeO-DMT or toad, tobacco purges — adds risk.

09

How do you prepare people?

Expect dietary guidance (some foods interact with MAO inhibitors), what the ceremonies will be like, and the right to sit one out.

10

What happens after I go home?

Integration — making sense of the experience in ordinary life — is where most of the healing is said to happen. Ask what follow-up support is included.

11

Can I leave at any time?

You should be free to stop, leave a ceremony, or leave the center, with a clear refund policy. Pressure to “push through” is a warning sign.

12

What's your legal status — and your history?

Ask what permits they hold, and verify them with the authority named. Then search the center and its healers' names with words like “death,” “abuse,” “lawsuit” and “investigation,” and read independent reviews — not just testimonials on their own site.

Good signs

  • Turns some applicants away after screening
  • Medical staff present during ceremonies
  • Small groups, several sober helpers
  • Written consent, touch and complaints policies
  • Answers hard questions plainly and in writing
  • Integration support after you leave

Walk away if

  • Nobody asks about your medications or health history
  • They promise to “cure” depression, addiction or trauma
  • They tell you to stop medication without your doctor
  • Several different substances are scheduled in one retreat
  • The healer works alone, at night, with no other staff
  • You're pressured to book fast, drink more, or keep secrets

Never stop a medication on a retreat's advice

Antidepressants (SSRIs, SNRIs, MAOIs), lithium, tramadol and some other opioids, stimulants and St John's wort can all interact dangerously with ayahuasca. Stopping antidepressants abruptly has its own serious risks. Any taper or washout belongs with your own prescriber — and for some people the safest answer is not to go.

Directory

Retreat centers & programs we researched

Long-running centers that publish their screening and safety practices, and where our searches (November 2026) did not turn up credible reports of a participant death or abuse at that center. That is a low bar, not a seal of approval: much of what's below comes from the centers' own materials. Run every one through the checklist above.

Takiwasi Center

Addiction treatment

Tarapoto, Peru · since 1992

A therapeutic community founded by French physician Jacques Mabit that combines traditional Amazonian medicine — ayahuasca ceremonies and plant dietas — with psychotherapy to treat drug and alcohol addiction. It also runs shorter plant-medicine retreats for people not in addiction treatment, including one for veterans and first responders.

They publish
A therapeutic team of psychologists and therapists working alongside curanderos; scheduled retreat dates for 2026–27.
Evidence
Its addiction program is the first study site of ATOP, an observational outcome study (no control group).1
Ask / verify
Admission criteria, medical clearance and costs. The residential addiction program is long and selective.

Temple of the Way of Light

Traditional Shipibo

Amazon rainforest near Iquitos, Peru

Multi-day retreats led by Shipibo healers, predominantly women (onanyabo) working alongside male healers, with groups kept to roughly two dozen guests.

They publish
An on-site nurse, first-aid-trained staff, emergency protocols, cell and radio communications, 24-hour transport to Iquitos and a contracted clinic in the city.
Ask / verify
These measures are self-reported. The site is remote: ask how long an evacuation actually takes, and how screening decisions are made.

Nihue Rao Centro Espiritual

Traditional Shipibo · long stays

Llanchama, about an hour from Iquitos, Peru · since 2011

Founded and led by Shipibo maestro Ricardo Amaringo. Known for longer stays — from one week up to six weeks — built around master-plant dietas, with ceremonies four nights a week.

They publish
Program lengths, ceremony schedule and prices on their booking pages.
Ask / verify
The booking pages we reviewed don't describe medical screening or on-site medical staff. Ask directly about both, and about the touch and consent policy. Long stays mean many ceremonies — be sure that's what you want.

Arkana Spiritual Center

Traditional Shipibo

Near Iquitos (Amazon) and the Sacred Valley near Cusco, Peru

One- to three-week programs led by Shipibo maestros and maestras, with several healers present at each ceremony.

They publish
A medical intake form and onboarding medical call, a named medical director, defibrillators and CPR-trained staff on site, and post-retreat integration programs.
Ask / verify

Soltara Healing Center

Traditional Shipibo

Playa Blanca (Nicoya Peninsula) and Scarlet Valley (Central Pacific), Costa Rica

Five-, seven- and twelve-night retreats with Peruvian Shipibo healers, alongside facilitators and therapists.

They publish
One-to-one confidential medical screening and an intake call, an on-site clinic, a stated Costa Rican Ministry of Health permit, and a year of follow-up integration support.
Ask / verify

Interfaith

Itacaré, Bahia, Brazil

A long-running center on Brazil's Bahia coast that pairs ayahuasca ceremonies with yoga, workshops, spiritual counseling and integration sessions.

Preparation guidance, and post-retreat counseling and integration.

Veterans program

U.S. nonprofit · places veterans with partner retreats

A veteran-founded nonprofit that places veterans and their spouses with partner retreats — historically ayahuasca centers abroad — and wraps them in preparation, peer support and integration. It reports more than 1,500 veterans and spouses served.

Why some well-known names aren't here

We left out centers where we found credible published reports of a participant death, sexual abuse or exploitation, or a regulator's warning about that specific center. Leaving a center out is not an accusation, and including one is not a clean bill of health. Popularity, polished marketing and “world's only” claims tell you nothing about safety.

Legal routes

Medically supervised and lawful options

If you're drawn to DMT as a healing process but don't want to travel to an unregulated retreat, these are the lawful doors that exist today. They are narrow.

01

Clinical trials

Trials of DMT and DMT-based drugs for depression and anxiety are underway. They screen carefully, cost nothing to join, and include medical monitoring — but you may receive a placebo. Search ClinicalTrials.gov for “DMT” or “ayahuasca” and filter by “recruiting.”

02

Ayahuasca churches

Santo Daime and UDV congregations practise lawfully in Brazil and under church-specific exemptions in the U.S. and Canada. Membership is a religious commitment, not a wellness booking.

03

Colorado

Colorado decriminalized personal use of DMT in 2022, but its licensed healing centers began with psilocybin only. The law allows the state to consider adding DMT after June 1, 2026 — check Colorado's Department of Regulatory Agencies (DORA) for the current status before assuming anything.

Support

Help before, during and after

  • Fireside Project (U.S.) — free psychedelic peer support by phone or text: 62-FIRESIDE (623-473-7433). Check current hours at firesideproject.org.
  • ICEERS El Faro Support Center — donation-based emotional support and integration after difficult experiences with ayahuasca and other plant medicines, plus medication-interaction questions beforehand. Not a crisis line. iceers.org
  • Chacruna Institute — the Ayahuasca Community Guide for the Awareness of Sexual Abuse, with a legal-resources companion for popular ayahuasca destinations.4
  • Your embassy — register your trip (U.S. citizens: the State Department's Smart Traveler Enrollment Program) and save the local emergency and embassy numbers before you go.
  • In a crisis — call local emergency services. U.S.: call or text 988. UK & ROI: Samaritans 116 123. For a sitter's step-by-step, see hard-trip first aid.
